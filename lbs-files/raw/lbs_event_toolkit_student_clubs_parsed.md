**Slide number:** 3

**Slide title:** 1. Event necessity & definition

**Content:**

### 1. Event necessity & definition

### Making your mark in a busy events landscape

Events can provide an opportunity for ideas to spark, connections to flourish, and our community to come alive. From faculty discussions to global conferences, each gathering has the potential to shape careers, forge partnerships, and advance knowledge.

Yet with over 800 events hosted annually across London Business School, standing out requires more than good intentions. The most memorable events, those that truly move our community forward, share one quality: they exist for a compelling reason. They fill a genuine need, create meaningful value, and leave participants changed in some way.

This toolkit will help you join that tradition of excellence. But first, let's ensure an event is the right solution for your goals.

#### Ask yourself “Why?”

Before diving into logistics, challenge yourself: Is an event truly the best way to achieve your objectives?

Start with your goals and audience needs, then select the most effective engagement method. Often, alternative solutions to events deliver better results with fewer resources.

### Match your need to the right solution

##### To share research findings or thought leadership

- Consider: Articles, blogs, LinkedIn posts, multimedia content

- Why: These formats reach your target audience through established channels without the overhead of event planning

##### To enable networking

- Consider: Leveraging existing gatherings, virtual meetups, or existing community platforms

- Why: More sustainable engagement than one-off events

- Top tip: Be mindful that the School has many established networking opportunities already, so look at what is happening across LBS

##### For updates and announcements

- Consider: Email bulletins, newsletter features, Teams/Hub posts, and short videos because these direct channels often achieve better engagement than events

- Why: Information reaches your audience when they're ready to receive it

##### When you're unsure of the best approach

##### If you're a student club, speak to the person who runs your channels.

If you don't know who to speak to, then you can attend one of the event planning drop-in sessions, which happen every Monday on Teams from 2-3pm - you simply book a 10 minute slot to ask any questions and get the support you need

- Event Planning Drop-in Session

If you cannot attend one of these session you can go to Editorial Planning (editorialplanning@london.edu), who can give you advice and help you identify the right channels and approach

##### The final strategic question

Before defaulting to an event, ask: "What specific value does bringing people together in person add that cannot be achieved another way?" If you struggle to answer, you've likely identified an opportunity for more effective communication.

### Define your purpose with precision

If an event remains your chosen path, clarity of purpose becomes essential. Successful events start with a vision that everyone, organisers, speakers, and attendees, can rally behind.

To ensure LBS events have value and to continuously improve and evolve, we require insightful data. Your commitment to proceeding with an event signifies an agreement to:

Why does the School require this data? This information will help you and future organisers make better decisions.

Remember: Setting clear, achievable objectives isn't just administrative - it's the foundation of a successful event. Plan your measurement approach before the event, not after. Then, before proceeding, honestly assess whether your event can deliver on its promises.

Ask yourself:

- Are these objectives realistic given our resources and constraints?

- Can we genuinely measure success against these goals?

- If we can't confidently meet these objectives, should we be running this event at all?

### Too often, event organisers set ambitious goals only to realise post-event that they were never truly achievable. Save yourself and your attendees the disappointment. If you can't see a clear path to meeting your stated objectives, it is time to reconsider whether an event is the right solution. Assess your return on investment

Every event requires an investment of time, money, and opportunity cost. Before proceeding, evaluate whether the expected returns justify these resources.

### Calculate true costs

- Direct costs: Venue, catering, technology, marketing

- Indirect costs: Staff time, volunteer hours, participant time away from other activities

- Opportunity costs: What else could these resources achieve? For example, while LBS venues are free for internal use, they have commercial value (e.g. Nuffield Hall can generate tens of thousands per day if hired externally)

### Measure potential returns

- Immediate outcomes: Registrations, attendance, engagement metrics

- Medium-term impact: New relationships, knowledge application, behaviour change

- Long-term value: Enhanced reputation, sustained partnerships

## Slide 12: Select the optimal event format

**Slide number:** 12

**Slide title:** Select the optimal event format

**Content:**

### Select the optimal event format

Your format should deliver your objectives within your constraints. Consider each option carefully:

#### Conference/Summit

- Best for: Thought leadership, major announcements, industry positioning

- Audience: 100+ diverse stakeholders

- Success factors: High-profile speakers, multiple perspectives, media appeal

- Lead time recommendation: 12+ months

- Budget implications: High (£15K-100K+)

#### Workshop/Masterclass

- Best for: Skill development, deep learning, behaviour change

- Audience: 15-40 committed participants

- Success factors: Interactive exercises, practical takeaways, expert facilitation

- Lead time recommendation: At least 3-4 months

- Budget implications: Medium (£3K-20K)

- Budget implications: Low-Medium (£1K-10K)

#### Networking reception

- Best for: Relationship building, community strengthening, celebration

- Audience: 50-200 with common interests

- Success factors: Facilitating connections, comfortable environment, clear purpose

- Lead time recommendation: At least 3-4 months

- Budget implications: Medium (£3K-20K)

#### Panel discussion

- Best for: Exploring debates, showcasing expertise, addressing controversies

- Audience: 40-150 seeking diverse viewpoints

- Success factors: Dynamic moderation, genuine disagreement, audience Q&A

- Lead time recommendation: At least 3-4 months

- Budget implications: Low-Medium (£1K-10K)

#### Fireside chat

- Best for: Intimate insights, personal stories, leadership lessons

- Audience: 30-100 seeking inspiration

- Success factors: Compelling speaker, skilled interviewer, relaxed atmosphere

- Lead time recommendation: At least 3-4 months

#### Hybrid experience

- Best for: Maximising reach, including global audiences

- Audience: Core in-person plus extended virtual

- Success factors: Technical excellence, equal participation, engaging both audiences

- Lead time recommendation: 6+ months

- Budget implications: High (add 40-60% to base format cost)

##### Additional challenges for hybrid events:

- Critical consideration: Delivering an experience beyond a basic Teams meeting needs extra budget. A quality hybrid event requires multiple cameras, camera operators, video mixing equipment, and technical staff.

- Coordination complexity: The extra moving parts present a significantly higher risk and a bigger investment of your time

- Ask yourself: Can you deliver an equally engaging experience for in-person and virtual audiences? If not, choose one format and excel at it

##### The format checklist

Before finalising, confirm your chosen format:

- [ ] Matches your primary objective

- [ ] Suits your audience preferences

- [ ] Works within budget and resource constraints

- [ ] Leverages available spaces effectively

- [ ] Differentiates from similar events

- [ ] Creates desired atmosphere

Design your team based on the events' scale and complexity. Not every role needs a dedicated person. Smaller events might have one person wearing multiple hats. What matters is that every responsibility has a clear owner.

## Slide 18: Master your timeline

**Slide number:** 18

**Slide title:** Master your timeline

**Content:**

### Master your timeline

Your timeline is your roadmap to success. Build it backwards from your event date, allowing buffer time for the inevitable surprises. Being ambitious and optimistic is great, but the planning fallacy is real. Events almost always take longer to plan than expected. Build in buffer time from the start - if you think something will take two weeks, plan for three. This is especially true when coordinating with multiple stakeholders or external suppliers.

### Timeline principles

- Start with non-negotiable deadlines and work backwards: There are some fixed activities that cannot move such as:

Catering orders (where catering is required)

Attending the Key Events Meeting (for events identified as Key Events)

Security clearance

- Critical path identification: Which tasks must happen in sequence? Which can run in parallel? Focus your energy on sequential bottlenecks that could delay everything else.

- Buffer building: Add 20% extra time for tasks involving external parties or busy stakeholders. Create contingency plans for critical dependencies

- Milestone visibility: Share key dates with all stakeholders early and often.

- The 48-hour rule: Make no major changes within 48 hours of the event unless it is absolutely critical.

For comprehensive suggestions of key milestones and suggested timings, see the event master list. This will help you plan when to:

- Book spaces on campus and speakers

- Launch registration and marketing

- Submit required forms and security clearances

- Finalise logistics and suppliers

### Anticipate challenges with a pre- mortem

Before problems arise, imagine they already have. The pre–mortem technique (imagining failure before it happens) helps identify risks while you can still prevent them.

#### Conduct your pre-mortem session

Gather your core team during your early planning stages:

1. Set the scene: "Imagine it's the day after our event, and it was a disaster. What went wrong?”

#### 2. Identifying potential risks: Each person writes

potential failure points (10 minutes)

#### 3. Share scenarios: Go round-robin without

judgment

#### 4. Categorise risks:

- High probability, high impact → Prevent at all costs

- High probability, low impact → Mitigate efficiently

- Low probability, high impact → Create contingency plans

- Low probability, low impact → Accept and monitor

#### Your risk register

It is often a good idea to create a living document that tracks, especially for larger scales event:

- Risk description

- Likelihood (1-5)

- Impact (1-5)

- Risk score (Likelihood × Impact)

- Mitigation actions

- Contingency plans

- Risk owner

- Review date

Once you have this document, you can use it for future events.

London Business School also has a Risk Assessment Matrix Audit form which you should complete for large scale events or events where you have a majority of external delegates.

## Slide 64: 9. Accessibility & inclusivity

**Slide number:** 64

**Slide title:** 9. Accessibility & inclusivity

### 9. Accessibility & inclusivity

### Create events where everyone belongs

At London Business School, diversity is our strength. Our community spans 155+ nationalities, multiple faiths, varied abilities, and diverse ways of thinking and learning. Your event should reflect and celebrate this richness.

Creating truly inclusive events isn't about ticking boxes - it's about anticipating needs, removing barriers, and ensuring every attendee can fully participate and contribute. This section helps you build accessibility into your event from the ground up, not as an afterthought.

#### Start with inclusive design principles

The most successful accessible events are those designed with inclusion from day one. Think of accessibility as expanding your reach, not limiting your options.

##### The universal design approach

When you design for the margins, you create better experiences for everyone:

- Captions help everyone: Not just those with hearing impairments, but also non-native speakers and anyone in a noisy environment

- Clear signage benefits all: From first-time campus visitors to those with cognitive differences

- Flexible formats work better: Offering both in-person and virtual options serves parents, international participants, and those with mobility challenges

- Quiet spaces help many: Supporting neurodivergent attendees while providing refuge for anyone needing a moment to recharge

### Build inclusion into your registration

Your registration form is often the first opportunity to understand and plan for attendee needs. Include this standard question:

"Do you have any accessibility requirements or dietary preferences we should know about? Please share any information that will help us ensure you can fully participate in this event."

#### Follow this with:

- A text field for specific requirements

- Assurance that information will be handled confidentially

- Contact details for further discussion if needed

Remember: Not everyone will disclose their needs at registration. Design for inclusion regardless of what's declared.

**Slide title:** Inclusive language

**Content:**

### Inclusive language

Communication sets the tone for belonging. Every word should reinforce that all attendees are valued.

### Registration and communications

#### Collecting personal information

- Use inclusive pronoun options (she/her, he/him, they/them, prefer not to say)

- Avoid binary gender options - include "non- binary," "prefer to self-describe," and "prefer not to say"

- Allow preferred name fields separate from legal names

- Make titles optional, not mandatory

#### Event communications

- Use gender-neutral language ("everyone" not "ladies and gentlemen")

- Avoid assumptions about attendees' backgrounds or experiences

- Include pronunciation guides for speakers' names

- Test all communications, and if in doubt, sense check with a colleague before sending

#### Creating inclusive presentations

- Use diverse imagery representing various ethnicities, abilities, ages, and backgrounds

- Ensure a minimum font size of 24pt for presentations. Avoid script fonts or those with all capital letters.

- Use high contrast colours (avoid red/green combinations)

- Steer away from overly bright or distracting palettes, clashing neon, flashing or changing colours, or patterns

- Avoid filling presentation with a wall of text. Too much text can overwhelm

- Describe visual content verbally for those with visual impairments

- Avoid cultural references that exclude international attendees

- Face your audience. Try not to turn your back or cover your face when speaking (e.g. when writing on a board), as this makes lip reading and understanding harder.
